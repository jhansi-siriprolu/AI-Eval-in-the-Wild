Lesson 01

What "evaluating a model" actually means

Before you download anything, get the vocabulary straight — it's what every model card, leaderboard, and research paper assumes you already know.

Three different questions people call "evaluation"

Capability

Can it get the right answer?

Multiple-choice knowledge tests, math word problems, code that must pass unit tests. Scored as accuracy — a plain percentage.

Fluency

Is the text itself plausible?

How surprised is the model by real text? Measured as perplexity — lower means the model predicted the actual next words more confidently.

Alignment

Does it behave the way you want?

Helpfulness, refusals, safety, following instructions. Usually scored by another model or a human — no clean formula.

This course focuses on the first two, because they're objective, reproducible on your own machine, and directly comparable to public leaderboards.

Why bother running this locally at all?

Public leaderboards (Hugging Face's Open LLM Leaderboard, lmarena.ai, etc.) already publish these numbers for thousands of models. Running your own evaluation matters when you need to know how a model performs on your data, in your quantization, on your hardware — numbers that no leaderboard can give you, because a 4-bit MLX build on an 8GB Air behaves differently from the full-precision model on a data-center GPU that produced the published score.

How scoring actually works under the hood

For multiple-choice benchmarks, the model is not asked to type "A", "B", "C", or "D". Instead, the evaluation harness computes the model's own probability of generating the full text of each answer option, and picks whichever option the model assigned the highest likelihood to. This is why it's called a log-likelihood evaluation — it reuses the same next-token-probability machinery you'll explore hands-on in Lesson 06.
